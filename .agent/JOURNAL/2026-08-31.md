## The counters, read at last

Thirty counters were instrumented under `__KT_METRICS__`, `summarize()` produced
p50 and p95, `readAllMetrics()` was exported, and `latency.mjs` was written to
read them. It needs real traffic on kick.com, so it never ran. Everything was
collected and nothing was read.

`metrics-offline` drives traffic through the real extension on a locally served
page and reads `chrome.storage.local` at the source, computing the percentiles
once from the raw reservoirs. `npm run metrics` builds with metrics, runs it,
and puts `dist` back. It refuses green when it read nothing, because a reader
that reports zero everywhere looks exactly like a perfect product.

## Three defects the numbers exposed

`(xd)+` was translated while every other repeated laughter form is skipped:
`x+d+` catches xd, xdd and xddd and misses xdxd, where jaja, haha, rsrs, huehue,
lolol, hehe and hihi are all handled. Corpus goes from 7 provider calls to 6.

**A batch declared one message's source language for all of them.** The
coalescer groups by target and nothing else, and `batchCall` built its joined
request as `{ ...reqs[0], text: joined }`. A request went out as `sl=ja`
carrying a Japanese line and an Arabic one, in a file whose own comment explains
that a wrong `sl` makes the engine translate from the wrong language. Disagreeing
hints now declare nothing.

**Malay and Hebrew were never identified**, two of the 42 offered languages, and
the listing sells Hebrew in its right-to-left claim. Malay because franc emits
`zlm` and the table knew `msa` and `zsm`; Hebrew because franc-min does not
cover it and the script pre-check had every other unambiguous script but not
that one. The first framing was wrong and the harness corrected it: an
unidentified language is not dropped, it goes out with `sl=auto`, so those
messages were translated all along. What the fix buys is a correct source
declared to the engine and a correct badge shown to the reader.

## The seven impurities, and one claim of mine that was false

An impurity is text that is nobody's speech and that, left in the message, does
one of three things: it skews language detection, it burns a provider call, or
it comes back translated in the reader's face. Forty-one cases, run through the
pipeline in its real order. Twenty-three behaved as intended before the pass
ended; nineteen did at the start.

**The correction first, because it was published.** Yesterday's commit said
eleven of fifteen keyboard smashes were already dropped through the English
rule. Measured on `detectLanguage` rather than reasoned about: zero of eleven.
All eleven reached the engine and three came back carrying a language,
`asdasdasd` Portuguese, `zxcvbnm` Spanish, `hjkhjkhjk` Dutch. I had conflated
`franc()` answering `und` with `detectLanguage` answering `'en'`. At the
`minLength: 3` floor franc answers with codes that map to nothing, so detection
returns undefined, and undefined drops nothing.

**The filter, and the criterion that was measured and thrown away.** The obvious
signal is the absence of vowels. It catches every smash and also `krk`, `prst`,
`smrt` and `vlk`, which are ordinary Czech words, and a filter that swallows
real speech in silence is worse than no filter. What was kept is the share of
adjacent letter pairs living on the same keyboard row. Against 15 smashes and 33
real words picked as the worst case: at 0.6 two false positives survive, at 0.65
and above none do, and all 15 are caught. 0.7 with a six-letter floor ships,
with margin on both sides. It is QWERTY-shaped and only judges a message of one
word, which is where the doubt costs least.

**Then it broke on stretched words, which my witnesses did not cover.** A held
letter necessarily lives on one row, so `siiiiiiii` and `NOOOOOO` carried the
signature exactly and were dropped as noise. What separates them from a smash is
the number of distinct letters, or a repeated group rather than a held letter:
`asdasdasd` is "asd" three times, `holaaaaa` is "hola" and a held a. Thirteen
stretched words sit in the battery now so that path stays closed.

**What the numbers say about the rest.** Transliteration is the worst, 0 of 5:
arabizi, greeklish and romaji all return no language, and romanised Russian
returns Indonesian. Platform text is 3 of 8, and its five failures are English
notices detected as French, Portuguese and Indonesian, but whether Kick even
puts them in a `div[data-index]` row is unestablished and that is what a real
corpus would settle. Code-switching 1 of 3, identity 3 of 5, no-language 5 of 6,
gaming slang 6 of 7.

Three defects in this pass were mine and not the product's: the published smash
claim, two mislabelled rows in my own grid, and a regex backreference the shell
turned into a control character, which made the group test match nothing and
quietly cost three smashes.

## The detector that won everything except the one thing

The previous pass named the experiment and named the number that would decide
it: not raw accuracy, but how many non-English messages end up classified
English and dropped in silence by `ignoreEnglish`. franc loses one in thirty.

The first duel had tinyld-light winning on every axis. 17 correct against 9,
4 silences against 9, 8 of 10 English recognised against 2, zero anglicised
against one, and 81121 bytes bundled against 174824, which is forty percent of
the whole injected script. I recorded that verdict and committed it.

Then I made the swap, and three tests failed. Not many out of 863, but the
interesting one was a Turkish line that stopped being sent at all. tinyld calls
`vay vay vay merhabalar` English, with a confidence of 0.16 against 0.10 for
Turkish, and `ignoreEnglish` drops it.

Adding those four cases to the corpus reversed the decisive number: franc 1 of
34, tinyld 5. The first corpus was too small to contain the cases that decide,
which is exactly why that number was chosen before the experiment rather than
after it.

**No threshold rescues it, and the reason is worth keeping.** tinyld returns a
confidence score where franc returns none, so the obvious repair is silence
below a bar. It makes things worse, because below the bar the ASCII fallback
calls the message English: at 0.15 it anglicises 20 of 34. Applying the bar only
to the English verdict takes 5 down to 4 and costs English recognition, and it
is flat from 0.2 to 0.8, which says tinyld is confidently wrong about those
three rather than hesitant.

So franc keeps the job. It is worse at almost everything and better at the one
thing that costs a reader their message. The forty percent of bundle that a swap
would have freed is not available at that price, and the margin has to be bought
somewhere else.

Reverted, dependency removed, 863 tests green, and the verdict I had already
published is corrected in the plan rather than quietly dropped.

## A change that shipped on one bench and was reverted on the next

`SHORT_TEXT_MAX` is 20, and eight of the thirteen wrong answers sat between 21
and 26 characters, out of the table's reach. Two benches of matched length said
raising it was worth doing: 13 foreign lines carrying a table word went from 10
right at 20 to 12 at 30 to 13 at 35, and 8 English chat lines carrying a table
word went from 2 wrong at 20 to 3 at 30 to 8 at 35. Length does not separate the
two, it lets them in together. franc does separate them, answering `eng` on 6 of
the 8 English lines and on none of the 13 foreign ones, so an explicit `eng`
cancelling the table's vote paid for the extension exactly: 12 of 13 right,
English damage unchanged at 2 of 8, 51 bytes, 23.5 to 38.9 microseconds per
message. `und` deliberately did not cancel the vote, since `und` is franc's
answer on short text and the table exists to replace it: franc says `zul` for
hola, `jav` for danke, `uzn` for obrigado, and `eng` for none of them.

I committed that. Then I asked the bench what it could not see, and the answer
was the message that changes language mid-sentence. Eight mixed lines through
the real order: at 20, three are killed before the call and none leaves with a
source language declared on one half; at 30, six are killed and four do. "merci
bro that was insane" is one French word in an English sentence, and at 30 it
leaves as `sl=fr`, which is the thing the file's own comment says makes the
engine translate from the wrong language, and it disappears for a French reader.
The veto rescues nothing there: franc answers `eng` on none of the eight.

A single foreign word inside another language is more common in a chat than a 25
character foreign sentence, so the bound goes back to 20. It sits there on this
measurement, not because 20 was ever measured as optimal. What survives the
revert is the witness: raising the constant now turns a test red on the source
language, which is the half of the damage that reaches the engine.

## Three categories rebuilt, and the tenth probe that accused working code

The deleted grid left identity at 3 of 5, no-language at 5 of 6 and slang at 6
of 7. Its cases are gone. What was actually missing from it is not the cases but
the definition of correct: a case without a written intent gets judged
afterwards, and afterwards it always gets judged in favour of what the product
already does.

So the intent went in first, per category. Identity: a handle, a channel name,
an emote name is nobody's speech and must not come back translated. No-language:
emoji, punctuation, digits, drawings must be dropped before the call. Slang: same,
plus one more reason, since translated it comes back as gibberish in the reader's
face. Twenty-one cases, run in the pipeline's real order.

**The first version of the probe accused the product on four of them, and the
product was right.** It judged the raw text. `parseKickContent` already strips
`@mentions` and URLs that carry a scheme, so `@handle`, two handles, a bare
`https://` URL and a handle followed by a URL all leave the parser as an empty
string and die on `minTextLength` before anything else looks at them.

I did not catch that from reading. I caught it from the fix. I had already moved
`isLinkOrMentionOnly` out of `composeLogic` into `filters`, wired it into
`prepare()`, added a `skipLinkOnly` reason to nine catalogues, and written the
pipeline test that was supposed to go red without the wiring. It went red with
it, saying "it is shorter than your 2 character minimum" where it expected the
new reason. Fourteen files reverted. The filter was never missing from the chat
path; the chat path never needed it.

That is the tenth accusation of healthy code over these passes, and the fourth
where the thing that exposed it was the witness rather than the reading.

**What actually survives**: the domain written without a scheme.
`kick.com/somechannel` is detected Spanish and `www.example.com/watch` French.
The parser's URL rule wants `https?://`, so those two reach the engine as text,
pay for a call, wear a wrong flag, and get skipped as already-in-your-language
for a Spanish or a French reader. `www.` would be safe to match on its own; a
bare domain needs a list of what looks like a TLD, and widening the parser's URL
rule also changes what renders as a link inside the row. Two decisions, and the
frequency for neither. Written down, not fixed.

The other two flagged cases were the probe's scoring, not the product:
"nova_ninety is cracked today" is an English sentence that happens to contain a
handle and is correctly read as English, and "W stream" is short English chat
where only the flag is wrong. The one real gap left in slang is `sheeeesh`:
`sheesh` is in the list, the stretched form is not, and `normalizeElongation`
collapses it to `shesh` rather than back to `sheesh`. It costs one call that the
post-call identical-text check then catches, so it stays.

## Un nom d'hote lu comme du rire japonais

kil a signale que `wwwww`, et meme `www`, valent le rire en japonais, pour
prevenir contre l'idee de reconnaitre `www.` comme un debut d'URL. La remarque
visait une regle a ne pas ecrire ; elle a nomme un defaut deja vivant, dans
l'autre sens.

La table des rires portait `ja` sur `/^w{3,}$/i` avec cette note : "three or
more, so it cannot be a bare www host". L'argument lit le message ENTIER. Le vote
porte sur les JETONS, et `detectByShortWords` decoupe sur les non-lettres, donc
`www.kick.com` donne www, kick, com et le premier vote seul.

Mesure : trois vrais noms d'hotes sur quatre sortaient `ja` **avec `sl=ja`**,
c'est-a-dire en demandant au moteur de traduire une URL depuis le japonais.
`www.kick.com`, `www.example.com`, `www.twitch.tv`. Le quatrieme,

## Le bulgare en lettres latines

L'item disait "une troisieme table du type `romanised.ts`". C'est faux, et c'est

Le chiffre qui a decide la forme. Des marqueurs batis par paradigme grammatical,
les interrogatifs, la particule du futur, les demonstratifs, les racines ou le
bulgare diverge du russe, prennent 10 des 20 lignes ecrites en meme temps qu'eux

existe. Un paradigme donne les mots d'un manuel. Un chat ecrit "mnogo dobre igra"
et "az sam tuk", qui n'en portent aucun. C'est le meme defaut que la table de
mots courts faite de salutations, vu avant livraison cette fois.

Les mots qu'un chat ecrit sont justement les ambigus : `dobre` est polonais, `az`
hongrois, `sam` un prenom anglais, `mnogo` et `smeshno` du russe romanise. Un
seul ne decide rien. DEUX dans la meme ligne decident : zero faux positif sur les
187 lignes des deux bancs du depot, en 19 langues, que ces listes n'ont jamais
regardees, et 3 des 4 lignes tenues a l'ecart contre 0 pour les forts seuls.

smeshno" pl vers bg, "az sam tuk" id vers bg, "ai stiga be" reste sv. Trois
deviennent justes, zero devient fausse. Sur les trois etiquetees, c'etait le
lecteur de CETTE langue qui perdait la ligne en "deja dans ta langue".

`sl` reste vide sur les huit, asserte et pas suppose : un marqueur romanise ne
doit jamais atteindre `confidentLanguage`, sinon on demande au moteur de lire du

## Le plancher de cinq lettres, respecte plutot que contourne

La table se donne un plancher : sous cinq lettres, un mot romanise entre en

dehors. Il a rejete neuf de mes marqueurs forts, `shte`, `kade`, `koga`, `tova`,

Ce que ca a coute, mesure : le rappel tenu a l'ecart ne bouge pas d'un point, 3

## Les sondes qui se sont trompees, quatre de plus

Douzieme et suivantes. Toutes rattrapees par un temoin, aucune par une relecture.

- **Le corps de formulaire.** Trois assertions du test DeepL rouges, sur un

## Deux chiffres cites de tete, deux faux

kil a corrige : les boutiques ne sont pas en 2.5.0. Lu sur les pages plutot que
repete :

  Chrome Web Store   2.9.2   mise a jour 2026-08-30   75 utilisateurs   290 Kio
  Firefox AMO        2.7.0   relue 2026-08-16          4 utilisateurs   publique

Donc Chrome est UNE version en arriere de 2.10.0 et AMO en est QUATRE, et les
deux boutiques ne sont pas au meme point. J'ai ecrit "les deux en 2.5.0, cinq
versions en arriere" dans `PLAN.md`, dans un commit pousse et dans mon rapport.

Et le troisieme tort est le plus interessant. L'item du taux d'installation
s'appuyait sur "la fiche n'a jamais ete mise a jour au-dela de 2.5.0" pour dire
que le 40.5 pour cent mesurait une page abandonnee. La description de la fiche
Chrome commence par "NEW IN 2.9.2". Elle a ete reecrite pour cette version. Le
chiffre n'est donc pas la ligne de base d'une page morte, c'est un verdict sur le
texte actuel, et l'experience a faire n'est pas de resoumettre mais de reecrire.

**D'ou ca vient.** D'une note de juin dans un fichier de memoire, reprise dans un
item du plan, puis dans un rapport, trois mois plus tard, sans etre re-derivee
une seule fois. Je l'ai lue, pas mesuree, et la difference entre les deux est
exactement ce que `feedback-provenance-discipline` dit : le sais-tu ou l'as-tu
herite.

**La reparation n'est pas de corriger la phrase.** Une version de boutique est
une page : elle change sans commit, donc aucune phrase dans un fichier suivi ne
peut rester vraie. Le prompt dit quoi faire quand un fait manque a `ETAT.json` :
le champ manquant est la premiere tache. `state.mjs` interroge donc les deux
boutiques maintenant, et `--texte` les imprime au demarrage :

    chrome       2.9.2  (2.9.2 contre 2.10.0 ici, 75 users, August 30, 2026)
    amo          2.7.0  (2.7.0 contre 2.10.0 ici, 4 users, 2026-08-16)

Trois details qui comptent. Les identifiants sortent de `README.md`, jamais
ecrits dans `state.mjs`, donc le champ ne peut pas finir par lire une fiche que
le projet ne lie pas. AMO a une API publique ; Chrome n'en a pas, donc sa version
est grattee a cote du libelle "Version", et un balisage qui bouge fait rendre une
note d'echec au lieu d'une valeur, parce qu'un grattage qui casse en silence est
la seule chose que ce fichier ne doit pas faire. Et `--hors-ligne` saute les deux
comme il saute deja la release.
